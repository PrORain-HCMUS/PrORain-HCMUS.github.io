CONGA, explained: sequential recommendation over very long histories

Why a recommender that reads your last 300 items still forgets who you are, and the three small changes (RoPE, doubly-stochastic streams, a neural memory) my RecSys '26 paper uses to fix it — including the parts that don't help.

Hoang-Vu Le · Nov 1, 2026 · 11 min read

Open a streaming app and it somehow knows the next film you'll want. Under the hood that is sequential recommendation: take a user's history, in order, and score every item in the catalogue as the possible next one. My co-authors and I wrote a paper about what happens when that history gets long — hundreds of interactions, not a handful. This post is the plain-language tour of it. The numbers below are the paper's; where I add my own reading, I say so.

The problem: a long memory the model can't use

The workhorse model here is SASRec (Kang & McAuley, 2018): a causal transformer over the user's item sequence. The paper points at two structural weaknesses, and they bite hardest on long histories such as MovieLens-1M, where the average user has 165.6 interactions (versus 8.9 on Amazon Beauty and 10.4 on Yelp).

  • Positions fall off a cliff. SASRec learns one position vector per index. Anything beyond the training window is a position the model has never seen — out-of-distribution by construction.
  • The window is a hard limit. Attention costs \(O(L^2)\) in sequence length, so in practice the model reads the latest 200–300 items and throws the rest away. One residual stream also has to carry both "what I watched yesterday" and "what I've always liked".

The 3D model above shows the shape of the fix. Recent items stay in the attention window. Older ones are not discarded but folded, one at a time, into a small fixed-size memory. The model reads both, and out comes a ranked top-K list.

The idea: three small, targeted changes

CONGA (COntinual Neural Gated Architecture) keeps the SASRec skeleton and swaps in three pieces, each aimed at one weakness. Figure 1 is the paper's own overview.

1 · RoPE: positions as rotations

Instead of adding a learned position vector, rotary embeddings (Su et al., 2021) rotate each query and key by an angle proportional to its position:

\[ \begin{aligned} q_t &= R_{\theta,t}\,W_Q\,e_t,\qquad k_s = R_{\theta,s}\,W_K\,e_s,\\ q_t^{\top}k_s &= f(e_t, e_s,\; t-s). \end{aligned} \]

Two properties matter. A rotation is orthogonal, so it never changes a vector's length, at any position. And the attention score depends only on the offset \(t-s\), so an offset seen in training is the same computation at position 900. I checked both in a few lines of NumPy (random 64-dimensional vectors, the usual \(\theta_j = 10000^{-2j/d}\)):

score(5, 2)      = -8.446152
score(905, 902)  = -8.446152     # same offset 3 -> identical
|q| before / after rotating to position 100000: 7.315344 7.315344

In the paper's ablation, swapping absolute positions for RoPE alone gives +3.9% NDCG@10 on ML-1M, and RoPE helps on all four datasets.

2 · KromHC: several residual streams, mixed doubly-stochastically

Rather than one residual stream, KromHC keeps \(M\) parallel streams and remixes them each layer. The mixing matrix is forced to be doubly stochastic — rows and columns all sum to 1 — so the mixing neither amplifies nor drains any stream, which is what the paper credits for balanced gradient flow. Exactness is cheap with a Kronecker product of two \(2\times2\) matrices:

\[ \begin{aligned} H_{\text{res}} &= M_1(a)\otimes M_2(b),\\ M(x) &= \begin{pmatrix}x & 1-x\\ 1-x & x\end{pmatrix},\quad a,b\in[0,1]. \end{aligned} \]

Two scalars in, an exact \(4\times4\) doubly-stochastic matrix out, no iterative Sinkhorn normalisation. Plugging in \(a=0.8,\ b=0.35\):

[[0.28 0.52 0.07 0.13]
 [0.52 0.28 0.13 0.07]
 [0.07 0.13 0.28 0.52]
 [0.13 0.07 0.52 0.28]]     row sums = col sums = 1

The number of streams is set by a simple rule on the corpus's mean sequence length: \(M=4\) when it is at least 50, otherwise \(M=2\) — which means four streams on ML-1M only.

3 · TITANS: a memory that learns while it reads

This is the part the 3D scene animates. TITANS (Behrouz et al., 2025) compresses the whole history into one \(d\times d\) matrix \(M_t\) — fixed size however long the history — and updates it online by gradient descent on its own prediction error:

\[ \begin{aligned} e_t &= M_{t-1}k_t - v_t,\\ S_t &= \mathrm{diag}(\eta_t)S_{t-1} - \mathrm{diag}(\theta_t)\,e_t k_t^{\top},\\ M_t &= \mathrm{diag}(1-\alpha_t)M_{t-1} + S_t,\\ y_t &= M_t q_t. \end{aligned} \]

Read it as: try to recall the value \(v_t\) from key \(k_t\); the miss \(e_t\) is the surprise; take a momentum step to reduce it, while slowly forgetting (\(\alpha_t\)).

CONGA adapts this to discrete item IDs and fuses it late, only at the last position, with one learned scalar \(\beta\):

\[ h_{\text{final}} = h_{\text{attn}}[L] + \beta\cdot y_{\text{mem}}[L'] ,\]

where attention sees the latest \(L=300\) items and the memory runs over up to \(L'=600\). Training is two-phase: Phase 1 trains the encoder alone; Phase 2 freezes it and trains only the memory. That is the "forgetting prevention" claim, and it is a structural one: the attention output is bit-for-bit what Phase 1 produced, and the only change to a score is the added \(\beta\cdot y_{\text{mem}}\) term. The paper is careful to say it is not a hard zero-shot guarantee — for short histories \(\beta\) is learned to be small.

What the experiments show

Everything is evaluated under full ranking — the true next item is ranked against the whole catalogue, with no sampled negatives — on four benchmarks and nine baselines, with leave-one-out splits and shared settings (\(d=64\), two blocks). The paper's headline: CONGA is first on 14 of 16 accuracy metrics. Here is NDCG@10 against the strongest baseline on each dataset:

DatasetAvg. historyCONGABest baselineRelative gain
ML-1M165.60.20120.1694 (WEARec)+18.77%

Steam14.20.06720.0646 (DuoRec)+4.02%
Beauty8.90.05940.0601 (FEARec)−1.16%

Read that table the way the paper does: the gain tracks history length. It is large where histories are long, and gone where they are short — on Beauty, CONGA loses two of its four metrics (HR@10 and NDCG@10) to FEARec. The paper's own advice for sparse corpora is to deploy the backbone-only variant.

Where does the gain actually come from?

This is the part worth slowing down for. The ablation adds one component at a time to a base encoder that already uses RoPE (row A0). On ML-1M:

ConfigHR@5NDCG@10
A0 base encoder (RoPE)0.24340.1984
A1 + KromHC0.24250.1988
A2 + SwiGLU0.24320.2007
A3 + TITANS (full CONGA)0.24350.2012

My reading of the paper's own numbers: the RoPE base A0 already scores 0.2434 HR@5, which is about 18.7% above the strongest baseline's 0.2051 — essentially the whole headline gain. The three later components add +1.41% NDCG@10 between them, and TITANS' own step is +0.25%. The paper says as much: the memory's main benefit is structural (a zero repetition rate and guaranteed no forgetting), with accuracy gains held back by the roughly 80-epoch Phase-2 budget. I also found no confidence intervals or multi-seed spreads in the paper, so differences in the third decimal place deserve caution.

The one place the memory visibly earns its keep is where the design says it should — long histories. Splitting the 6,040 ML-1M users by history length:

+0.04% for users with 50 or fewer items, +0.15% for 51–150, +0.72% beyond 150. A clean monotone trend, and exactly the one a long-range memory should produce — but the absolute lift for the longest users is 0.1521 → 0.1532.

One user, up close

The project page walks through a single ML-1M user (id 2244) with 362 recorded interactions. The held-out true next item is The Godfather, sitting about 357 interactions in the past — well outside a 300-item window — while the most recent items are comedies and animation. The memory-less models follow the recent mood; CONGA's memory recovers the older action/crime pattern.

One honest caveat: this is a single illustrative user, chosen by the authors to show the mechanism. It is evidence of how the memory can help, not of how often.

Limits, plainly

  • It is built for long histories. KromHC and TITANS contribute negatively at short mean lengths (the paper says so for roughly \(\bar L \le 50\)); on Steam the TITANS step costs −11.3% NDCG@5, and on Yelp KromHC alone dips NDCG@10 by 6.5% before later components recover it.
  • Beauty is a loss on two metrics. The recommendation there is the backbone-only model.
  • The baseline A0 isn't published SASRec. It is SASRec with RoPE, chosen so the ablation isolates the other components. A fair choice, but it means the ablation cannot show how much RoPE alone contributes beyond the +3.9% control.
  • A small accuracy–diversity trade-off. On ML-1M the memory nudges head bias up (5.58% → 6.36%) and intra-list diversity down (0.6528 → 0.6235). Coverage@10 barely moves (74.68% → 74.80%).
  • Memory cost and cold start. The memory is \(O(d^2)\) per user, which limits batch size at large \(d\); users with fewer than about ten interactions get no memory benefit.
  • Results come from the comparison the authors set up. The strongest baseline on three of the datasets is WEARec, an arXiv preprint from late 2025, and every baseline uses the same \(d=64\), two-block configuration.

Run it or read it

The official PyTorch code is at github.com/nguyentuongbachhy/CONGA (MIT). The README's quick start builds the fused CUDA extensions (RoPE, KromHC, SwiGLU, TITANS) and trains on ML-1M:

cd code/src
uv sync --frozen --no-install-project
uv pip install --no-build-isolation \
  -e ./modules/rope -e ./modules/mhc -e ./modules/mhcv2 -e ./modules/swiglu -e ./modules/titans

uv run python main.py --dataset ml-1m --lr 0.001 \
  --hidden_units 64 --num_blocks 2 --num_heads 2 --train_dir exp_conga

Baselines train through the same entry point by changing --model_type (SASRec, BSARec, BERT4Rec, GRU4Rec, FMLP-Rec, DuoRec, FEARec, WEARec). I haven't re-run training for this post — the commands above are the README's, and the extensions need an NVIDIA GPU toolchain. A good reading order is the RoPE module first (the smallest), then the TITANS module, where the recurrence above becomes a register-tiled kernel.
