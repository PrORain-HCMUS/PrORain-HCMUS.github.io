Why doubly-stochastic mixing keeps residual streams balanced

The intuition behind my RET 2026 paper, with a toy experiment you can reproduce in twenty lines of NumPy.

Hoang-Vu Le · Nov 1, 2026 · 9 min read

A standard transformer has one residual stream: each block reads it and adds something back. A growing family of architectures keeps several parallel streams and lets the model mix them between blocks. That raises an innocent-looking question: what constraint should the mixing matrix obey? My paper Not All Residuals Are Equal (RET 2026) and the KromHC component of CONGA are both about one answer, doubly stochastic. The proof in the paper is sharper than anything here, so this post is just the intuition I use to think about it, plus a small experiment.

Setup

Stack \(s\) streams into \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{s\times d}\). Between blocks we mix them with a learned \(s\times s\) matrix \(M\):

\[ Y = M X, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial X} = M^{\top}\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}. \]

Forward, each output stream is a combination of the input streams. Backward, the gradient is pushed through the transpose. Over \(\ell\) layers the forward map is a product \(P = M_\ell \cdots M_1\) and the backward map is \(P^{\top}\). Whatever constraint we put on \(M\) has to behave well for the product, in both directions.

Three candidates

1. Unconstrained

Nothing stops \(\|M\|_2\) from sitting a little above 1, and a product of 64 such matrices can reach \(1.2^{64}\approx 10^5\). Unconstrained mixing is the version I would expect to need careful initialisation and some luck.

2. Row-stochastic

Make every entry non-negative and every row sum to 1, which is what a row-wise softmax gives you. Now each output stream is a convex combination of the inputs, which is reassuring for the forward pass. But look at the backward pass. Take

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},\qquad M^{\top}\begin{pmatrix} g_1 \\ g_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} g_1 + g_2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

This matrix is row-stochastic, yet stream 2 receives no gradient at all while stream 1 receives the sum of both, and \(\|M\|_2 = \sqrt{2}\). In general \(\|M\|_2\) can reach \(\sqrt{s}\). Row sums control the forward direction. They say nothing about how gradient mass is shared out in the backward direction.

There is a cleaner way to see the long-run behaviour. For a product of positive row-stochastic matrices, \(P = M_\ell\cdots M_1\) tends to a rank-one matrix \(\mathbf{1}\pi^{\top}\), where \(\pi\) is a stationary distribution. Then \(P^{\top} G = \pi\,(\mathbf{1}^{\top}G)\): stream \(i\) ends up receiving a share of the gradient proportional to \(\pi_i\). Streams with small \(\pi_i\) are starved. Nothing in the constraint makes \(\pi\) uniform.

3. Doubly stochastic

Now also require every column to sum to 1. Then \(M\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}\) and \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}M=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\), and three things follow.

  1. Mass is conserved in both directions. Forward, \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}Y=\mathbf{1}^{\top}X\): the average over streams is unchanged. Backward, \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}M^{\top}=(M\mathbf{1})^{\top}=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\): the total gradient is unchanged too. The stream-average direction passes through every layer exactly, in both passes.
  2. The norm cannot blow up. By the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem, a doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices, \(M=\sum_k \theta_k P_k\). Then \(\|Mx\|_2 \le \sum_k\theta_k\|P_kx\|_2 = \|x\|_2\), so \(\|M\|_2\le 1\). The transpose is doubly stochastic as well, so the same holds backward, and products of doubly stochastic matrices are doubly stochastic, so it holds for any depth.
  3. No stream is favoured. The stationary distribution of a doubly stochastic chain is uniform, so the long-run gradient share is \(\pi_i = 1/s\) for every stream.

The smallest case shows all of this at once. A 2×2 row-stochastic matrix has rows \((a,\,1-a)\) and \((b,\,1-b)\), so it is a point in the unit square. Its column sums are \(a+b\) and \(2-a-b\), so it is doubly stochastic exactly when \(a+b=1\): a diagonal segment whose two ends are the two permutation matrices. On that segment \(\|M\|_2=1\). Away from it the norm climbs, and the corner \(a=b=1\) is the matrix from the counter-example above, with norm \(\sqrt{2}\). I computed the norm on a 400×400 grid: the maximum over the square is \(\sqrt{2}\), the maximum along the segment is 1.

My informal reading of “balanced gradient flow” is exactly these properties: nothing amplified, nothing starved, no stream privileged. The paper proves the precise statement, including the converse direction (that the condition is also necessary) and the geometry behind it. I will not pretend to reproduce that here.

Getting there: Sinkhorn

Row-stochastic costs one division per row. Doubly stochastic is harder, because fixing the columns breaks the rows. Sinkhorn’s answer is to alternate: divide every row by its sum, then every column by its sum, and repeat. For a matrix with strictly positive entries this converges to a doubly stochastic matrix, which is what makes it a usable layer: positivity from an \(\exp\), then a few normalisations.

The figure runs it on the matrix from the snippet at the end (seed 0: \(\exp(Z)\) with a boost on the diagonal, rescaled to total mass 4). The raw row sums are 1.24, 1.66, 0.59 and 0.51, the column sums 1.12, 1.44, 0.78 and 0.67. One step makes every column sum exactly 1 and pulls the rows to 1.07, 1.04, 0.95 and 0.93. Because each step ends on the columns, the columns are exact and the rows carry the leftover error. The worst row error is 0.070, 0.052, 0.038, 0.029 and 0.022 after steps 1 to 5, so it shrinks by roughly a quarter per step on this diagonal-heavy matrix, and it is 1.5·10⁻⁸ after 60. I have not derived that rate; a matrix closer to a permutation is the slow case, so it is worth measuring on your own logits before choosing the number of steps.

That is the animation at the top of the page. Its right-hand panel is the quantity from point 3 above: at every instant I take the row-normalised matrix, find its stationary distribution \(\pi\) (power iteration), and draw it as bars. For the raw matrix those bars are uneven, and they level out to \(1/s\) as the sums are pulled to 1.

A toy experiment

Four streams (\(s=4\)), width \(d=64\), a random unit-norm gradient at the output, backpropagated through up to 64 mixing layers with no sub-layers in between. Each layer’s matrix comes from one of three recipes: unconstrained is the identity plus Gaussian noise of scale \(1/\sqrt{s}\); row-stochastic is a row softmax of a random logit matrix with a boost on the diagonal; doubly stochastic is the same logits pushed through 60 Sinkhorn normalisation steps. I report medians over 300 random draws.

At 64 layers the unconstrained gradient is about \(10^7\) times its starting size and one stream receives roughly 5.6 times the gradient of another. Row-stochastic mixing keeps the norm bounded (about 0.53) but settles at a stubborn imbalance of about 2.3 between the most- and least-served stream, which is the \(\pi\) of its random chains showing through. Doubly stochastic mixing stays bounded and drives the imbalance to 1.00 within about 16 layers. I also checked the norm claim directly: over 2000 random doubly stochastic draws the largest spectral norm was 1.00005, the slack being finite Sinkhorn iterations, while random row-stochastic draws reached 1.27.

The medians hide what a single chain looks like, so here are three individual draws (seeds 3, 7 and 11, 64 layers, same logits for both recipes). With row-stochastic mixing the biggest stream gets 1.9 to 2.4 times the gradient norm of the smallest, and which stream wins is arbitrary. With doubly stochastic mixing all four sit at 0.25.

What the toy leaves out

  • The 0.5 is not a bug. After many layers the doubly stochastic gradient has norm \(1/\sqrt{s}=0.5\): only the stream-average component survives, because repeated mixing washes out differences between streams. In a real network the sub-layers keep injecting stream-specific signal, and the mixing is usually initialised near the identity. Balance is a property of the pipes, not a promise that the network learns well.
  • No sub-layers, no nonlinearities. This isolates the mixing matrices, and nothing else. It is an illustration of the mechanism, not evidence about any trained model.
  • Exact versus approximate. Sinkhorn projection is only approximately doubly stochastic after finitely many steps (hence the 1.00005). A parametrisation that is doubly stochastic by construction, such as the Kronecker-product one in KromHC, avoids that slack, and it is why I like it.

Where this sits

DeepSeek’s mHC (manifold-constrained hyper-connections) constrains the mixing matrices in a similar spirit. Sebastian Raschka’s roundup, Recent Developments in LLM Architectures, covers it among other residual-stream redesigns. My own use of the idea is in sequential recommendation: CONGA uses doubly stochastic stream mixing, and the RET paper validates it on four sequential-recommendation benchmarks.

Takeaway. Row sums of 1 protect the forward pass only. Making the mixing matrix doubly stochastic conserves mass and bounds the norm in both passes and makes every stream’s long-run gradient share equal. Those are the properties I mean by “balanced”, and the paper’s theorem is the precise version.

Reproduce it

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
s, d, L = 4, 64, 64

def sinkhorn(K, it=60):
    for _ in range(it):
        K = K / K.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        K = K / K.sum(0, keepdims=True)
    return K

def mixing(kind):
    Z = rng.normal(0, 1, (s, s)) + 3 * np.eye(s)
    if kind == "free":
        return np.eye(s) + rng.normal(0, 1, (s, s)) / np.sqrt(s)
    E = np.exp(Z)
    return E / E.sum(1, keepdims=True) if kind == "row" else sinkhorn(E)

G = rng.normal(size=(s, d)); G /= np.linalg.norm(G)
P = np.eye(s)
for _ in range(L):
    P = mixing("ds") @ P           # try "free" and "row"
g = P.T @ G                        # backprop through all L layers
r = np.linalg.norm(g, axis=1)
print(np.linalg.norm(g), r.max() / r.min())
