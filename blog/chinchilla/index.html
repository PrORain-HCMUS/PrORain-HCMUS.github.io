Chinchilla in one derivation

Where “about 20 tokens per parameter” comes from, and why I trust the derivation more than the number.

Hoang-Vu Le · Nov 1, 2026 · 8 min read

In 2022 DeepMind trained a 70B-parameter model on 1.4T tokens and beat their own 280B-parameter Gopher, which had used roughly the same compute. An entire generation of models, it turned out, had too many parameters and too little data. I keep hearing the conclusion as a slogan, “20 tokens per parameter”. These are my notes on where that slogan comes from, because it turns out to be a ten-line calculation.

The setup: a loss with three terms and a budget

Hoffmann et al. model the final loss of a transformer trained with \(N\) parameters on \(D\) tokens as

\[ L(N, D) \;=\; E \;+\; \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} \;+\; \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} . \]

Each term has a plain reading. \(E\) is the irreducible loss, the entropy of the text itself, which no model can beat. \(A/N^{\alpha}\) is what you lose because the model is finite. \(B/D^{\beta}\) is what you lose because the data is finite. The budget is the usual approximation that training costs about six FLOPs per parameter per token, so

\[ C \;\approx\; 6\,N D . \]

The question: for a fixed \(C\), which split between \(N\) and \(D\) minimises \(L\)?

The derivation

Spend the budget completely and eliminate \(D = C/(6N)\). The loss becomes a function of \(N\) alone:

\[ L(N) \;=\; E + A N^{-\alpha} + B\,6^{\beta} C^{-\beta}\, N^{\beta}. \]

Set the derivative to zero:

\[ -\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1} + \beta B\, 6^{\beta} C^{-\beta} N^{\beta-1} = 0 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; N^{\alpha+\beta} = \frac{\alpha A}{\beta B\, 6^{\beta}}\; C^{\beta}. \]

Raise both sides to the power \(1/(\alpha+\beta)\) and write it the way the paper does:

\[ N_{\mathrm{opt}} = G\Big(\tfrac{C}{6}\Big)^{a},\qquad D_{\mathrm{opt}} = G^{-1}\Big(\tfrac{C}{6}\Big)^{b},\qquad a = \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta},\;\; b = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},\;\; G = \Big(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}} . \]

Is that stationary point really the minimum? Multiply the derivative by \(N^{1-\beta}\) and it becomes \(-\alpha A N^{-\alpha-\beta} + \text{const}\), which is strictly increasing in \(N\). It crosses zero exactly once, from negative to positive, so the minimum is global and unique. (Convexity would be the lazy argument, but \(N^{\beta}\) is concave for \(\beta \lt 1\), so I did not use it.)

Three things the formula says

  • The exponents only depend on \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). The constants \(A\), \(B\), \(E\) and the 6 shift the lines on a log–log plot but not their slope.
  • \(a + b = 1\). That is just \(ND = C/6\) again.
  • The sign of \(\alpha-\beta\) decides what happens to tokens per parameter. Since \(D/N = C/(6N^2) \propto C^{(\alpha-\beta)/(\alpha+\beta)}\), the ratio is constant exactly when \(\alpha=\beta\). “20 tokens per parameter” is therefore not a law of nature. It is what you get when the two exponents happen to be close.

A picture of the trade-off

For each compute budget, plot the predicted loss against model size, with data size set by \(D = C/6N\). Small models are starved of capacity, large ones of data, so each curve is a U, and its bottom moves right as the budget grows.

With these constants every optimum has the same \(D/N \approx 21.4\), as the algebra predicts when \(\alpha=\beta\). I deliberately did not paste the fitted values from the paper: the main text prints the exponents but not the constants, so I would have had to quote them from memory.

Three measurements, one answer

The paper does not rely on the parametric form alone. It estimates \(a\) and \(b\) three ways, over 400+ training runs. The numbers below are from its Table 2:

  • Minimum over training curves: \(a=0.50,\ b=0.50\).
  • IsoFLOP profiles (fix the budget, sweep model size, find the bottom of the U): \(a=0.49,\ b=0.51\).
  • Parametric fit of the loss above, then the formula: \(a=0.46,\ b=0.54\).

All three say to grow parameters and tokens roughly in equal proportion. Kaplan et al. had found \(a=0.73\), \(b=0.27\), i.e. spend most of the extra compute on a bigger model. Because \(N\) and \(D\) scale as \(C^a\) and \(C^b\), a tenfold bigger budget means \(10^{a}\) times more parameters and \(10^{b}\) times more tokens:

The 70B / 1.4T arithmetic

Using \(C \approx 6ND\): Chinchilla is \(6\cdot 70\text{B}\cdot 1.4\text{T} \approx 5.9\times10^{23}\) FLOPs and Gopher is \(6\cdot 280\text{B}\cdot 300\text{B} \approx 5.0\times10^{23}\). Same ballpark, and the paper describes them as the same budget. The ratio \(1.4\text{T}/70\text{B}=20\) is where the slogan comes from. Note it is an implication of one training run and a fit, not a sentence in the paper.

What the paper’s own constants say

Everything above used constants I chose so that \(\alpha=\beta\). The real fit is less tidy. Besiroglu et al. print, in their Equation 4, the Approach 3 values Hoffmann et al. fitted, which they recovered from the TeX source of the paper: \(E=1.6934\), \(A=406.4\), \(B=410.7\), \(\alpha=0.3392\), \(\beta=0.2849\). Feed them through the same formulas:

  • \(a=\beta/(\alpha+\beta)=0.456\) and \(b=0.544\), matching the 0.46 / 0.54 in the list above.
  • Now \(\alpha>\beta\), so \(D/N\) is no longer constant. It scales as \(C^{(\alpha-\beta)/(\alpha+\beta)}=C^{0.087}\): every tenfold increase in compute raises the ratio by a factor \(10^{0.087}\approx 1.22\).
  • At Chinchilla’s own budget, \(C=5.9\times10^{23}\), that gives \(N\approx 41\text{B}\) and \(D\approx 2.4\text{T}\), about 59 tokens per parameter, nowhere near the 20 that was used. (Besiroglu et al. read a figure nearer 70 off their curve; I have not tracked down the gap, but both are far above 20.)

Besiroglu et al. refit the same functional form to data they reconstructed from the paper’s plot and got \(E=1.8172\), \(A=482.01\), \(B=2085.43\), \(\alpha=0.3478\), \(\beta=0.3658\). Now \(\beta>\alpha\) and the exponent is \(-0.025\), so the ratio drifts slightly down. At the same budget it gives \(N\approx 73\text{B}\), \(D\approx 1.3\text{T}\), a ratio of 18.4, which is almost exactly the model DeepMind trained. Tiny shifts in \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) move the answer from 59 to 18, which is why I say the slogan is fragile and the derivation is not.

Why I hold the number loosely

  • The exponents are fragile. Kaplan’s 0.73 and Chinchilla’s 0.5 are not a contradiction in the mathematics. They come from different experimental setups, the learning-rate schedule being a major one. The allocation exponent is sensitive to procedure, not a constant of nature. Lilian Weng’s Scaling Laws, Carefully is a good tour of how much those procedural choices matter.
  • Approach 3 looks too good. Its reported interval for \(a\) is absurdly narrow, and a replication attempt (Besiroglu et al.) found the published estimates inconsistent with Approaches 1 and 2, then rederived values that agree with them. Their Figure 5 shows it at a glance: the ratio implied by the published constants (green) climbs with compute, while the refit (blue) stays near 20 and its 80% band easily contains the dashed 20 tokens-per-parameter line.
  • Compute-optimal for training is not optimal for use. If a model will serve billions of requests, a smaller model trained on more tokens than the rule suggests can be cheaper overall. Chinchilla answers “what is the best model for this training budget”, not “what should I deploy”.
Takeaway. Minimising \(E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta}\) under \(C = 6ND\) gives \(N_{\mathrm{opt}} \propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{opt}} \propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}\). Equal exponents mean a constant tokens-per-parameter ratio. The derivation is solid; the particular number depends on a fit, and fits are fragile.
