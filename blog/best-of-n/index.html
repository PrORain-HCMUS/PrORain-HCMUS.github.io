Thinking longer, voting more: the maths of why test-time compute saturates

Best-of-n, majority voting and long chains of thought all spend compute at answer time. Each one has a ceiling you can write down.

Hoang-Vu Le · Nov 1, 2026 · 9 min read

If a model is right 60% of the time, can I just sample it more and get to 90%? Reasoning models made "spend more compute at inference" the default answer to everything. These are my notes while studying it, and what I took from them is that every flavour of test-time compute has a ceiling you can derive on a page. Here are three: best-of-n, voting, and simply thinking for longer.

Notes, not results. Everything below is textbook-style algebra I re-derived and checked numerically while studying. The models are deliberately toy-sized; their value is in the shape of the answer, not the exact numbers.

1. Best-of-n pays a logarithmic KL bill

Draw n samples from a base policy \(\pi\), score each with a reward model, keep the best. The resulting policy \(\pi_n\) is not \(\pi\) any more, and the distance between them is exactly what you "spent" to get a higher reward. How much is it?

Assume the reward \(r(y)\) has a continuous distribution under \(\pi\) (no ties). Let \(u = F(r(y))\in[0,1]\) be the reward's quantile. The best of \(n\) draws has the largest quantile, whose density is \(n u^{n-1}\), while under \(\pi\) the quantile is uniform. So the density ratio is \(\pi_n(y)/\pi(y) = n\,u^{n-1}\) and

\[ \mathrm{KL}(\pi_n\,\|\,\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{u\sim n u^{n-1}}\big[\log n + (n-1)\log u\big] = \log n - \frac{n-1}{n}, \]

because \(\mathbb{E}[\log u] = -1/n\) when \(u\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)\). Plugging in numbers:

For large n the bill is basically \(\log n\): every doubling costs about \(\ln 2 \approx 0.69\) nats more. Two caveats. First, with ties (a binary verifier gives only 0 or 1) the formula is no longer exact; Beirami et al. show it is an upper bound on the true KL and not tight in general, so read the table as a ceiling for those cases. Second, KL is only the cost side. Whether the extra reward is worth it depends on the reward model, and a noisy grader makes the benefit curve bell-shaped in \(\log n\): past some point you are optimising the grader's mistakes.

(While re-checking, I noticed my own notes had 3.22 for \(n=64\). The correct value is \(\ln 64 - 63/64 = 3.17\).)

2. Voting saturates because samples are not independent

Majority voting works by Condorcet's logic: if each sample is right with probability \(p>\tfrac12\) and samples are independent, the vote gets arbitrarily good. But samples from the same model on the same question share its blind spots. Model that with a correlation \(\rho\) between the indicators "sample \(i\) is correct". The variance of the vote share is

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}\big[1+(n-1)\rho\big] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{1+(n-1)\rho}\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;\frac1\rho . \]

With \(\rho = 0.05\), a modest correlation:

Going from 64 to 256 samples buys 3 effective samples for 4× the cost. At \(\rho=0.08\) and \(n=16\), the variance is already \(1+15\times0.08 = 2.2\) times what the independent formula promises. This is also why raising the sampling temperature helps up to a point: it lowers \(\rho\). Past that point you are sampling worse answers.

One honest limit: this treats the samples as exchangeable with a single \(\rho\) (which must satisfy \(\rho\ge -1/(n-1)\)). In reality \(\rho\) is really "how much of the error is question-level difficulty", and that varies wildly across questions. What it does explain is the stubborn flat top of the accuracy-vs-samples curve: voting removes sampling noise, not the model's systematic mistakes.

3. Thinking longer can make things worse

The third kind of test-time compute is sequential: let the model keep reasoning, revising, saying "wait". Model each extra reasoning round as one step of a two-state Markov chain over {correct, wrong}. A round fixes a wrong answer with probability \(a\) and breaks a correct one with probability \(b\). If \(A_k\) is the accuracy after \(k\) rounds,

\[ A_{k+1} = A_k(1-b) + (1-A_k)\,a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A_k = A_\infty + (A_0 - A_\infty)(1-a-b)^k,\qquad A_\infty = \frac{a}{a+b}. \]

It is a linear recurrence with slope \(1-a-b\), whose fixed point is \(A_\infty\). Three consequences fall straight out:

  • It saturates. The gap to \(A_\infty\) shrinks geometrically, independent of the budget. With \(a=0.245,\ b=0.105\) the slope is 0.65, and a model starting at 0.25 is within one point of the ceiling after \(\ln(0.01/0.45)/\ln 0.65\approx 9\) rounds.
  • The ceiling is not 1. It is \(a/(a+b)\): the more often a round breaks a correct answer, the lower the ceiling.
  • Thinking longer hurts when \(A_0 > a/(a+b)\). If the model is already right more often than the long-run rate, extra rounds drag it down toward \(A_\infty\).

The third point matches the "overthinking" people report on easy problems, and it is an argument for a reasoning model having an off switch or an effort setting rather than a single "think more" dial. It also gives a break-even rule of thumb: buy inference compute only on questions where the starting accuracy sits below \(a/(a+b)\).

The caveat: \(a\) and \(b\) are not constants. They depend on the question, on whether there is an external check (a test suite, a calculator), and on whether the model can tell its own errors from its own answers. Self-correction without an external signal is the regime where \(b\) tends to be non-negligible. What I would want to measure before spending the compute is \(a\) and \(b\), per question type.

Takeaway. Best-of-n costs \(\log n-\tfrac{n-1}{n}\) nats and leans on the grader; voting caps out near \(1/\rho\) effective samples; sequential thinking converges to \(a/(a+b)\) and can fall to it from above. "More compute" is only a plan if you know which of the three ceilings you are standing under.
