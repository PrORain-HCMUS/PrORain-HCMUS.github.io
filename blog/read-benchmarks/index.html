How to read an LLM benchmark without fooling yourself

A two-point gap on a leaderboard can be noise, pass@64 near 100% says little about reliability, and Elo is one line of gradient descent.

Hoang-Vu Le · Nov 1, 2026 · 9 min read

A new model scores 82.1 on a benchmark and last month's scored 80.4. Is it better? Before the number goes in a slide, I want to know whether 1.7 points is signal. These are my study notes on the five checks I now run on any evaluation result: sampling error, pass@k, Elo, judge bias and Goodhart.

Notes, not results. All numbers below are computed by me from the formulas, not taken from any particular leaderboard.

1. A score is a sample mean, so it has an error bar

If a model answers each of \(N\) independent questions correctly with probability \(p\), the measured accuracy has standard error

\[ \mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{2\sqrt N}. \]

For an MMLU-sized test, \(N\approx 14{,}000\) and \(p\approx 0.8\): \(\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.16/14000}\approx 0.34\) points, so a 95% interval is about \(\pm 0.66\). A 1.7-point gap on that benchmark is meaningful. On a 500-question benchmark with the same \(p\), \(\mathrm{SE}\approx 1.8\) points and the interval is \(\pm 3.5\). Comparing two models that were scored independently multiplies the SE by \(\sqrt2\), to about 2.5, so the gap must exceed roughly 5 points to clear the 95% bar. A 1.7-point gap there is indistinguishable from noise.

Two refinements. Scoring both models on the same questions lets you use a paired test, which is much tighter because question difficulty cancels. And this error bar covers only which questions were drawn, not run-to-run sampling randomness, prompt phrasing or contamination, so it is a lower bound on honest uncertainty.

Turn the formula around to ask how many questions a claimed gap \(d\) needs. For two independent models the 95% bar is \(d > 1.96\sqrt{2p(1-p)/N}\), i.e.

\[ N \;>\; 2p(1-p)\Big(\frac{1.96}{d}\Big)^{2}. \]

With \(p=0.8\) and \(d=0.017\) that is \(0.32\times 115.3^2\approx 4{,}250\) questions: a 14,000-question benchmark clears it comfortably, a 500-question one is far short. Clearing the bar is not the same as detecting the gap reliably. For 80% power you need \((1.96+0.84)^2\) in place of \(1.96^2\), about 8,700 questions. And the popular shortcut of checking whether the two individual error bars overlap is stricter than it looks, because it compares \(2\times1.96\,\mathrm{SE}\) rather than \(1.96\sqrt2\,\mathrm{SE}\): the same gap would need about 8,500 questions before the whiskers part. (That is the moment the scene above turns from red to teal.)

If you would rather not trust a formula, resample. Treat the scored test set as the population, draw \(N\) questions with replacement, rescore, and repeat. For 0/1 outcomes one resample is just a Binomial draw. I did that 200,000 times for two models whose observed scores are 82.0 and 80.4 on 500 questions (the nearest counts to 82.1 and 80.4) and for 82.1 and 80.4 on 14,000. The rank flips in about 27% of the 500-question resamples and in none of the 14,000-question ones.

2. pass@k measures coverage, not reliability

For code and maths, people report pass@k: the chance that at least one of \(k\) samples is correct. The naive way is to generate exactly \(k\) samples and check, which has high variance. The standard fix (introduced with HumanEval in Chen et al.) is to generate \(n\ge k\) samples, count the \(c\) correct ones, and compute

\[ \widehat{\text{pass@}k} = 1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}} . \]

It is unbiased because \(\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\) is exactly the probability that a uniformly random \(k\)-subset of your \(n\) samples contains no correct one. Example: \(n=20\) samples, \(c=3\) correct, \(k=5\): \(1-\binom{17}{5}/\binom{20}{5} = 1-6188/15504 = 0.60\). In code, use the numerically stable product form:

def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    if n - c < k:
        return 1.0
    # 1 - prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n} (1 - k/i)
    p = 1.0
    for i in range(n - c + 1, n + 1):
        p *= 1.0 - k / i
    return 1.0 - p

Now the interpretation trap. With independent samples and per-sample success \(p\), \(\text{pass@}k = 1-(1-p)^k\), which races to 1 as \(k\) grows. A model that solves a problem only 20% of the time has \(\text{pass@}1 = 20\%\) but \(\text{pass@}64 = 1-0.8^{64}\approx 0.9999994\). That is 99.99994%, not a number to be impressed by: it says a correct solution exists somewhere in the distribution, not that the model will give it to you.

The informative quantity is the gap \(\text{pass@}k-\text{pass@}1\): that is the part of the benchmark where the model could succeed but usually doesn't, which is exactly what a verifier or RL training can try to convert into reliability. (In an earlier version of my own notes I had written 99.97% for this example. The arithmetic gives 1 − 6.3×10⁻⁷, so I fixed it.)

3. Elo is gradient descent on Bradley–Terry

Arena-style leaderboards turn pairwise human votes into ratings. The Bradley–Terry model says model \(i\) beats \(j\) with probability \(\sigma(\theta_i-\theta_j)\), and the Elo expected score is the same thing in disguise: with \(s = 400/\ln 10\),

\[ E_i = \frac{1}{1+10^{(r_j-r_i)/400}} = \sigma\!\big((r_i-r_j)/s\big). \]

For one match with outcome \(S_i\in\{0,1\}\), the negative log-likelihood has gradient \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial r_i = -\tfrac1s(S_i-E_i)\), so the familiar update

\[ r_i \leftarrow r_i + K\,(S_i - E_i),\qquad r_j \leftarrow r_j - K\,(S_i - E_i) \]

is one step of stochastic gradient descent with learning rate \(\eta = Ks\). Example: \(r_i=1000\) against \(r_j=1200\) gives \(E_i = 0.24\); if \(i\) wins with \(K=32\) it gains \(32\times0.76\approx 24\) points.

Reading Elo as SGD explains its three well-known quirks. \(K\) is a learning rate (speed against noise). The result depends on vote order: take two models with a 2–2 record where A wins the first two matches and B the last two, and A ends at 994.7; reverse the order and A ends at 1005.3, an 11-point difference from nothing but ordering. And a single SGD run gives no error bars. That is why arenas moved to fitting Bradley–Terry by maximum likelihood on all votes at once (order-independent, with bootstrap confidence intervals).

4. LLM judges have a position bias

When a model grades two answers \(a,b\), it tends to favour one slot. Suppose it picks the first slot with probability \(q(a,b)=\pi(a,b)+\delta\), where \(\pi\) is the "true" preference and \(\delta\) is a bias that does not depend on content. Ask twice with the order swapped, and average:

\[ \hat\pi(a\succ b)=\tfrac12\big[q(a,b) + 1 - q(b,a)\big] = \tfrac12\big[(\pi(a,b)+\delta) + 1 - (1-\pi(a,b)+\delta)\big] = \pi(a,b). \]

The \(\delta\) terms cancel. Swapping costs two judge calls instead of one and removes the additive part of the bias. It does not remove verbosity bias, self-preference or style bias, which are not about position, so the swap is necessary, not sufficient.

5. The winner's curse (Goodhart, statistically)

Suppose 20 candidate models are all exactly equally good, but each measured score carries independent noise with standard deviation \(\sigma\) (the SE from section 1). The expected maximum of 20 standard normals is about 1.87 (I checked by simulation), so the top-ranked model is expected to look \(1.87\sigma\) better than it is. On the 14,000-question benchmark that is \(\approx 0.6\) points; on a 500-question one it is \(\approx 3.4\). Choose the best of many noisy scores and you select, in part, for luck. Add real optimisation pressure on the metric itself (training on it, tuning prompts to it) and it stops measuring what it used to measure. The habit that follows: report the interval, pre-register which metric you will pick, and keep a held-out set that nobody tunes against.

Takeaway. Before believing a benchmark delta: compute \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\), ask whether the models were compared on the same questions, know that \(\text{pass@}k\) at large \(k\) is coverage, remember that Elo is order-dependent SGD, swap the order for judges, and discount the winner of a crowded field.
