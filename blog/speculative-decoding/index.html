Speculative decoding: the acceptance rate is 1 − TV, so train for TV, not KL

A small model drafts, a big model checks, and the output is provably unchanged. Why the speed-up is a total-variation quantity, and what that implies for how drafters should be trained.

Hoang-Vu Le · Nov 1, 2026 · 8 min read

How can a small, sloppy model make a big, careful model faster — without changing a single token the big model would have said? That sounds like a free lunch, so I wanted to see exactly where the catch is. These are notes while studying speculative decoding; none of the core results are mine (see the reading list at the end), but the last section, on training objectives, is the part I found most useful.

The procedure in one paragraph

Autoregressive decoding is slow because every token needs a full forward pass of the big target model with distribution \(p\). Speculative decoding adds a cheap draft model with distribution \(q\). The draft proposes a token \(x \sim q\). The target, which can score many positions in one pass, accepts it with probability \(\min\!\big(1, p(x)/q(x)\big)\). If it rejects, we sample a replacement from the residual distribution

\[ r(x) \;=\; \frac{\max\big(p(x) - q(x),\,0\big)}{\sum_{y}\max\big(p(y) - q(y),\,0\big)} . \]
def step(p, q, rng):
    x = rng.choice(len(q), p=q)                 # draft proposes
    if rng.random() < min(1.0, p[x] / q[x]):    # target verifies
        return x
    r = np.maximum(p - q, 0); r /= r.sum()      # residual
    return rng.choice(len(r), p=r)

Why the output is exactly \(p\)

Take a four-token vocabulary with \(p = (0.50, 0.30, 0.15, 0.05)\) and \(q = (0.40, 0.40, 0.10, 0.10)\). The probability that token \(x\) is emitted is "proposed and accepted" plus "rejected and resampled":

\[ \Pr[x] \;=\; \underbrace{q(x)\min\!\big(1,\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\big)}_{=\,\min(p(x),\,q(x))} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\, r(x), \qquad \alpha=\sum_y \min(p(y),q(y)). \]

Here \(\min(p,q) = (0.40, 0.30, 0.10, 0.05)\), so \(\alpha = 0.85\). The leftover mass \(1-\alpha = 0.15\) times the residual \(r = (2/3,\,0,\,1/3,\,0)\) is \((0.10, 0, 0.05, 0)\). Add them up and you get \((0.50, 0.30, 0.15, 0.05) = p\). In general \((1-\alpha)\,r(x) = \max(p(x)-q(x),0)\), and \(\min(p,q) + \max(p-q,0) = p\) — that one line is the whole lossless proof. (I also checked it by simulating 400,000 draws: the empirical frequencies match \(p\) to within about \(10^{-3}\).)

The acceptance rate is overlap, which is \(1-\mathrm{TV}\)

The same number \(\alpha\) is the chance that a proposed token survives, and it is the overlap of the two distributions. Since \(\min(a,b) = \tfrac12\big(a+b-|a-b|\big)\),

\[ \alpha(p,q) \;=\; \sum_x \min\big(p(x),q(x)\big) \;=\; 1 - \tfrac12\sum_x |p(x)-q(x)| \;=\; 1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q). \]

In the example, \(\mathrm{TV} = \tfrac12(0.10+0.10+0.05+0.05) = 0.15\), so \(\alpha = 0.85\). Not KL, not cross-entropy — total variation, exactly.

From one token to a block

In practice the draft proposes \(\gamma\) tokens and the target verifies them all in one pass. Verification stops at the first rejection. If all \(\gamma\) are accepted, the target has already computed the distribution at position \(\gamma+1\), so it emits one free bonus token. If each position is accepted independently with probability \(\alpha\), the expected number of tokens per round is

\[ \mathbb{E}[\tau] \;=\; \sum_{i=0}^{\gamma}\alpha^{i} \;=\; \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha}. \]

With \(\alpha=0.85\) and \(\gamma=4\): \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = (1-0.85^5)/0.15 \approx 3.71\) tokens per round. And no matter how long the block, the ceiling is \(1/(1-\alpha) = 1/\mathrm{TV}\), which is \(6.7\) tokens here. A drafter with \(\mathrm{TV}=0.15\) can never deliver more than about 6.7 tokens per target pass.

The average hides a lopsided distribution. A round ends early with probability \(\alpha^{i}(1-\alpha)\) after exactly \(i\) accepted drafts, and ends with the bonus token only if all \(\gamma\) survive. For \(\alpha=0.85,\ \gamma=4\):

Picking the draft length

Drafting isn't free. Say a round costs one target pass (\(c_t = 1\)) plus \(c_d\) per drafted token, with \(c_d/c_t = 0.12\) — a number I picked for illustration. Throughput, in tokens per unit of target time, is

\[ T(\gamma) \;=\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[\tau]}{c_t + c_d\,\gamma}. \]

For \(\alpha=0.85,\ \gamma=4\) that is \(3.71/1.48 \approx 2.5\times\) plain decoding. Because \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) saturates while draft cost keeps growing linearly, there is an optimal \(\gamma^{*}\), and it moves a lot with \(\alpha\):

The original paper plots the same trade-off the other way round: the best integer \(\gamma\) as a function of \(\alpha\), one curve per cost ratio \(c=c_d/c_t\). For \(c=0.1\) and \(\alpha\approx0.85\) the red curve sits at \(\gamma=7\); my own arithmetic for \(c=0.12\) agrees (\(\gamma^{*}=7\), \(2.64\times\)), and shows how flat the top is, since \(\gamma=4\) already gets \(2.5\times\). The staircase also explains why the optimum jumps so quickly at high \(\alpha\): the last few percentage points of acceptance buy a lot of draft length.

This is why serious systems adapt \(\gamma\) per workload: code and other structured text accept well (high \(\alpha\), long blocks pay off), while open-ended chat accepts poorly (short blocks). Chen et al. measured exactly that on a 70B Chinchilla target with a 4B drafter: on HumanEval (code) the average accepted fraction stays high as the draft grows, while on XSum (summarisation) it drops faster and the wall-clock time stops improving after about three drafted tokens. They report a 2–2.5× speed-up depending on the task.

So what should we train the drafter on?

The default is to distil the drafter with a KL loss, \(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)\), because it is smooth and well understood. But the speed-up is governed by \(\mathrm{TV}\). How are the two related?

One direction holds. Pinsker's inequality gives \(\mathrm{TV}(p,q) \le \sqrt{\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)}\), so \(\alpha \ge 1-\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}/2}\). Drive KL to zero and acceptance goes to one.

The other direction fails. Let \(p = (1-\varepsilon,\ \varepsilon)\) and \(q = (1,\ 0)\). Then \(\mathrm{TV} = \varepsilon\) — tiny, so \(\alpha = 1-\varepsilon\) — while \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = \infty\) because \(q\) gives zero mass to something \(p\) can say. A small KL is sufficient for good acceptance, but it is not necessary, and minimising it can spend capacity on exactly the wrong things.

That gap matters when the drafter is too small to represent \(p\), which is the normal case. Then "closest in KL" and "closest in TV" are different models, and only the second maximises acceptance. In a toy simulation from my own study notes (a vocabulary of 128 tokens, a drafter restricted to a rank-\(r\) family, fitted once by KL and once by TV), the picture was:

  • tiny capacity (\(r=2\)): both fits are equally bad, about +1% throughput for TV;
  • middle capacity (\(r=48\)): acceptance rises from 0.744 (KL) to 0.861 (TV); at \(\gamma=7\) that is \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) of 3.54 versus 5.02, about +42% throughput;
  • full capacity (\(r=128\), the family can express \(p\) exactly): the two objectives share the same optimum and the gap vanishes.

So the benefit of the right objective is a hump: zero at both extremes, largest in the realistic middle. The exponential amplification inside \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) is why a twelve-point gain in \(\alpha\) turns into forty-odd percent in speed.

Caveats

  • That experiment is a small synthetic one, not a production drafter, and I have not reproduced it on real language models.
  • TV is non-differentiable where \(p(x)=q(x)\). A naive subgradient fit gets stuck and under-reports the TV solution; in my simulation I smoothed \(|z|\approx\sqrt{z^2+\epsilon}\) and annealed \(\epsilon\to 0\). Anyone using a TV-style loss for real needs a similar plan, or a mixed loss with a small KL term for stability.
  • My notes also mention recent drafter-training work that moves away from pure KL; I haven't verified those papers, so I won't name them here.
  • \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) above assumes independent acceptance per position. Real acceptance falls with position, which also argues for weighting early positions more in the training loss.
Takeaway. Speculative decoding is lossless because \(\min(p,q)+\max(p-q,0)=p\). Its acceptance rate is exactly \(1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\), and its speed-up ceiling is \(1/\mathrm{TV}\). KL is a sufficient proxy only when the drafter can nearly match the target; when it cannot, the objective you train on matters, and it should be the one you are scored on.
